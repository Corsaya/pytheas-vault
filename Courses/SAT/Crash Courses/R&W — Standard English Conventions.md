# R&W — Standard English Conventions Crash Course

**This is the highest-priority crash course of the 8.** Standard English Conventions is
~26% of the R&W section (11–15 questions) per
[[../Research/Official SAT Structure and Content Research (2026-08-07)]], and it's your
**confirmed weakest content area** — both the Dec/Mar official reports and the 2026-08-07
diagnostic agree on this. Covers C1–C11 from
[[../Foundations Knowledge Check#Reading & Writing — Standard English Conventions (~26%, 11–15 Q)|the Foundations Knowledge Check]].
Every rule below gets a precise statement, correct/incorrect examples, and the specific
trap the SAT loves to build into this rule — read all of it, not just the ones that feel
unfamiliar, since several of these have sneaky exceptions even when the base rule feels
obvious.

---

### C1 — Subject-verb agreement, incl. interrupting phrases

Already fully covered — don't re-derive it, just use the existing lesson:
[[../Diagnostic Gap Lessons (2026-08-07)#Lesson 3 — Standard English Conventions: subject-verb agreement with interrupting phrases]]

Quick summary: the verb agrees with the **true subject**, not the noun sitting closest to
it. Phrases like *along with, as well as, together with, in addition to, accompanied by*
sit between subject and verb without changing the subject's number — mentally delete the
whole phrase and check what's left. "And" is the only thing that genuinely makes a
compound subject plural.

### C2 — Comma splice / run-on identification and fix

**What this actually tests:** whether two independent clauses (each one a complete
sentence on its own) have been joined with strong-enough punctuation.

**The rule:** Two independent clauses (IC + IC) can be joined only by:
1. A period — `IC. IC.`
2. A semicolon — `IC; IC.`
3. A comma + a FANBOYS conjunction (**F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o) — `IC, and IC.`

A comma *alone* between two ICs is a **comma splice**. No punctuation at all between two
ICs is a **run-on / fused sentence**. Both are errors.

- **Correct (comma + FANBOYS):** "I finished my homework, so I went outside to play."
- **Correct (semicolon):** "I finished my homework; I went outside to play."
- **Incorrect (comma splice):** "I finished my homework, I went outside to play."
- **Incorrect (run-on):** "I finished my homework I went outside to play."

**Trips people up:** a comma splice *reads* fine out loud — your ear doesn't flag it the
way a run-on does — so you can't proofread this by ear alone; check whether both halves
could stand as their own sentence, and if so, confirm the punctuation between them is
strong enough.

### C3 — Independent vs. dependent clause: the 4 IC/DC punctuation rules

**What this actually tests:** whether the punctuation between clauses matches whether each
clause could stand alone as a full sentence.

An **independent clause (IC)** has a subject and verb and is a complete thought on its own.
A **dependent clause (DC)** has a subject and verb too, but starts with a word that makes it
incomplete on its own (*because, although, when, since, if, while,* etc.) — it needs an IC
attached to make sense.

**The 4 rules:**
1. `IC. IC.` — two complete sentences, period between them.
2. `IC; IC.` — semicolon between two closely related complete sentences.
3. `IC, FANBOYS IC.` — comma + coordinating conjunction.
4. `DC, IC.` when the dependent clause comes **first** (needs a comma) — but `IC DC.` when
   the dependent clause comes **second** (usually **no** comma).

- **Correct:** "Although the storm ended, we stayed inside." (DC first → comma)
- **Correct:** "We stayed inside although the storm continued." (DC second → no comma)
- **Incorrect:** "Although the storm ended we stayed inside." (missing comma after leading DC)
- **Incorrect:** "We stayed inside, because the storm continued." (unnecessary comma before a trailing DC)

**Trips people up:** the comma rule flips depending on clause order — DC-first always
wants a comma, but DC-second (especially after *because*) almost never does. The SAT
loves inserting an unneeded comma right before a trailing "because" clause.

### C4 — Apostrophes: possessive vs. plural, its/it's, whose/who's

**What this actually tests:** whether an apostrophe signals possession/contraction, versus
being wrongly added to a simple plural.

**The rules:**
- Possessive singular: add `'s` — "the dog's bone" (one dog owns the bone).
- Possessive plural (word already ends in s): add just `'` — "the dogs' bones" (multiple dogs own bones).
- Plain plural: **no apostrophe** — "I saw three dogs."
- `its` = possessive ("belonging to it") — never has an apostrophe.
- `it's` = contraction of "it is" or "it has" — always has an apostrophe.
- `whose` = possessive ("belonging to whom").
- `who's` = contraction of "who is" or "who has."

- **Correct:** "The company announced its earnings." / "It's raining."
- **Incorrect:** "The company announced it's earnings." (should be *its*)
- **Correct:** "Whose book is this?" / "Who's coming to the party?"
- **Incorrect:** "Who's book is this?" (should be *whose*)

**Trips people up:** `its`/`it's` and `whose`/`who's` are the two most-tested apostrophe
pairs precisely because the "add apostrophe = possessive" instinct is backwards for these
words — for pronouns, the apostrophe version is always the contraction, never the possessive.

### C5 — Pronoun-antecedent agreement, incl. singular "they"

**What this actually tests:** whether a pronoun matches its antecedent (the noun it refers
back to) in number, and — for the digital SAT specifically — whether you know singular
"they" is accepted, not treated as an error.

**The rule:** A pronoun must match its antecedent's number. Plural antecedent → plural
pronoun (*they/their*). Singular antecedent → singular pronoun. **Important exception the
digital SAT follows:** for a singular but gender-unspecified/generic antecedent (*everyone,
someone, a person, each student, each employee*), **"their/they" is accepted as correct** —
you do not need "his or her." The one thing that still matters is **consistency**: don't
refer to the same antecedent with different pronouns mid-sentence, and don't use "they/their"
for a specific, known, non-person antecedent (a company, a book, a committee acting as one
unit still typically takes "it/its").

- **Correct (singular they, accepted):** "Each employee is required to submit their timesheet by Friday."
- **Correct (true plural):** "The scientists published their findings."
- **Incorrect (inconsistent):** "A person should always trust their own instincts, but they must also listen to it." (switches from *they* to *it* for the same antecedent)
- **Incorrect (wrong pronoun for a non-person singular):** "The committee submitted their final report." (a committee acting as one body takes *its*)

**Trips people up:** don't reflexively mark "their" as wrong just because the antecedent is
grammatically singular — check whether the antecedent is a generic/indefinite person (their
is fine) versus a specific singular thing/group-as-a-unit (needs *it/its*).

### C6 — Pronoun case (who/whom, I/me, subjective/objective)

**What this actually tests:** whether a pronoun is doing the action (subjective case) or
receiving it (objective case).

**The rule:**
- **Subjective** (subject of a verb): I, he, she, we, they, **who**.
- **Objective** (object of a verb or preposition): me, him, her, us, them, **whom**.
- `who` = subject ("Who called you?" — *who* does the calling).
- `whom` = object ("To whom did you give the letter?" — *whom* is the object of "to").

- **Correct:** "She and I went to the store." (subject of *went*)
- **Incorrect:** "Her and me went to the store."
- **Correct:** "The gift is between you and me." (object of preposition *between*)
- **Incorrect:** "The gift is between you and I."

**Trips people up:** in a compound ("Sam and I" / "my colleague and me"), isolate just the
pronoun and drop the other person — "I went to the store" sounds right, "Me went to the
store" sounds wrong — your ear catches it once the compound is stripped away, even when the
full compound sentence tricks it.

### C7 — Verb tense consistency across a sentence/passage

**What this actually tests:** whether verb tenses shift without a logical, signaled reason.

**The rule:** Stay in one tense unless the timeline of events genuinely requires a shift —
and if it does, the sequence has to make logical sense (e.g., past perfect for something
completed *before* another past event).

- **Correct:** "She walked to the store and bought milk." (past, past — consistent)
- **Incorrect:** "She walked to the store and buys milk." (past then present — unjustified shift)
- **Correct (justified shift, past perfect before simple past):** "By the time the guests arrived, Maria had prepared the entire meal." (*had prepared* happened before *arrived*)

**Trips people up:** not every tense shift is an error — if the sentence has a real reason
for two different times (an earlier-completed action needs past perfect, or context words
like "yesterday"/"tomorrow" signal a real time change), the shift is correct. Only flag a
shift that has no logical basis.

### C8 — Modifier placement (dangling/misplaced modifiers)

**What this actually tests:** whether an introductory modifying phrase is immediately
followed by the noun it's actually describing.

**The rule:** A modifying phrase at the start of a sentence (usually ending in a comma)
must be followed directly by the noun performing the action described in that phrase. If
the noun right after the comma couldn't logically do what the phrase describes, the
modifier is "dangling."

- **Correct:** "Exhausted after the long flight, Jenna found the hotel bed incredibly comfortable." (*Jenna* is the one who's exhausted)
- **Incorrect (dangling):** "Exhausted after the long flight, the hotel bed felt incredibly comfortable to Jenna." (a bed can't be exhausted)

**Trips people up:** check literally who/what sits right after the introductory comma — if
that noun can't logically perform the action in the modifying phrase, it's wrong no matter
how natural the sentence sounds when read quickly.

### C9 — Parallel structure in a list or comparison

**What this actually tests:** whether every item in a list (joined by *and/or*) or a
comparison (joined by *than/as*) shares the same grammatical form.

**The rule:** All items in a list or comparison must match grammatically — all gerunds
(*-ing*), all infinitives (*to + verb*), all nouns, etc. Mixing forms breaks parallelism.

- **Correct:** "She likes hiking, swimming, and biking." (all gerunds)
- **Incorrect:** "She likes hiking, swimming, and to bike." (gerund, gerund, infinitive — mismatched)
- **Correct:** "It is better to give than to receive." (both infinitives)
- **Incorrect:** "It is better to give than receiving." (infinitive vs. gerund)

**Trips people up:** the SAT usually plants the mismatch in the **last** item of the list —
check the final item's grammatical form against the first two before assuming the sentence
is fine.

### C10 — Non-essential vs. essential clause commas (which/that, appositives)

**What this actually tests:** whether a describing clause is *essential* (needed to
identify which noun is meant) or *non-essential* (extra info that could be deleted), since
that determines both punctuation and word choice.

**The rule:**
- **Essential/restrictive** (identifies *which one* — can't be removed without losing
  meaning): **no commas**, use **that** for things, **who** for people.
- **Non-essential/nonrestrictive** (adds extra info about something already identified):
  **commas required**, use **which** for things, **who** for people. This also covers
  appositives (a renaming phrase) — non-essential ones get commas.

- **Correct (essential):** "Employees who complete the training by Friday will receive a certificate." (no commas — specifies *which* employees)
- **Correct (non-essential):** "My uncle Robert, who moved to Denver last year, visits us every summer." (commas — there's only one uncle Robert; the clause is extra info)
- **Incorrect:** "Employees, who complete the training by Friday, will receive a certificate." (commas wrongly suggest *all* employees complete it — changes the meaning)
- **Incorrect:** "The book which I borrowed is on the table." (no commas + *which* — should be *that*, no commas, since it's essential)

**Trips people up:** *which* is only correct **with** commas, and *that* never takes commas
— if you see "which" with no commas, or "that" with commas, that's almost always the error.
Test it by asking: can I delete this clause without losing track of which noun we mean? If
yes → non-essential (commas, *which/who*). If no → essential (no commas, *that/who*).

### C11 — Colon and semicolon usage (beyond IC/DC — lists, explanations)

**What this actually tests:** whether a colon is set up correctly (complete sentence before
it) and whether a semicolon is used to separate list items that already contain commas.

**The rule:**
- **Colon:** what comes *before* it must be a complete independent clause. What follows can
  be a list, an explanation, or an elaboration — but never put a colon directly after
  "such as," "including," or a verb like "was" with no object yet.
- **Semicolon (beyond joining two ICs):** also used to separate items in a list when the
  items themselves already contain commas, to avoid confusion.

- **Correct (colon):** "The chef's philosophy was simple: use fresh ingredients and never rush the process." ("The chef's philosophy was simple" is a complete IC on its own)
- **Incorrect (colon):** "She packed: a passport, a map, and sunscreen." ("She packed" is not complete without an object — not a full IC)
- **Correct (semicolon in a complex list):** "I've lived in Portland, Oregon; Austin, Texas; and Reno, Nevada." (semicolons separate city/state pairs that already have internal commas)

**Trips people up:** always check that the words immediately before a colon could stand
alone as a full sentence — if they can't, the colon is wrong no matter how natural the list
after it looks.

## Mini-Diagnostic

One question per skill, C1–C11, real digital-SAT Conventions format. Answer key with
reasoning is below — don't peek until you've answered all 11.

**C1.** The museum's collection of rare manuscripts, alongside its extensive photography archive, ______ far larger than most visitors realize.
A) are
B) is
C) were
D) have been

**C2.** The bridge was closed for repairs, drivers had to take a long detour.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) repairs; drivers had to take a long detour.
C) repairs drivers had to take a long detour.
D) repairs, drivers, had to take a long detour.

**C3.** Because the rain continued through the night the outdoor concert was cancelled.
Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
A) NO CHANGE
B) night, the outdoor concert
C) night the, outdoor concert
D) night; the outdoor concert

**C4.** The company reported that ______ profits had doubled since last year.
A) it's
B) its
C) its'
D) it is

**C5.** Each employee is required to submit ______ timesheet by Friday.
A) their
B) them's
C) it's
D) there

**C6.** The award was presented to my colleague and ______ after months of hard work.
A) I
B) me
C) myself
D) mine

**C7.** By the time the guests arrived, Maria ______ the entire meal.
A) prepares
B) has prepared
C) had prepared
D) will prepare

**C8.** Exhausted after the long flight, the hotel bed felt incredibly comfortable to Jenna.
Which choice best fixes the underlined portion?
A) NO CHANGE
B) Exhausted after the long flight, Jenna found the hotel bed incredibly comfortable.
C) The hotel bed, exhausted after the long flight, felt incredibly comfortable to Jenna.
D) Jenna's hotel bed felt incredibly comfortable, exhausted after the long flight.

**C9.** The workshop taught participants how to write clear emails, how to organize meetings efficiently, and ______.
A) how to give effective presentations
B) giving effective presentations
C) effective presentations were given
D) to give effective presentations well

**C10.** Employees ______ complete the training by Friday will receive a certificate.
A) who
B) whom
C) , who,
D) , which,

**C11.** The chef's philosophy was simple ______ use fresh ingredients and never rush the process.
A) :
B) ;
C) ,
D) (no punctuation, just a space)

### Answer key

- **C1 — B) is.** True subject is "collection" (singular); "alongside its extensive photography archive" is a non-essential interrupting phrase, not part of the subject.
- **C2 — B) repairs; drivers had to take a long detour.** The two clauses are both independent, so they need a semicolon (or period, or comma+FANBOYS) — a plain comma (A) is a splice, no punctuation (C) is a run-on, and an extra comma (D) doesn't fix the splice.
- **C3 — B) night, the outdoor concert.** A dependent clause ("Because the rain continued through the night") coming first needs a comma before the independent clause that follows.
- **C4 — B) its.** Possessive form of "it" — never takes an apostrophe.
- **C5 — A) their.** "Each employee" is a singular, generic antecedent — singular "their" is accepted; the other options are either not real words or the wrong part of speech.
- **C6 — B) me.** Object of the preposition "to" ("to my colleague and me") — objective case, not subjective ("I") or reflexive ("myself").
- **C7 — C) had prepared.** Past perfect is needed for an action (preparing the meal) completed before another past action (the guests arriving).
- **C8 — B).** Fixes the dangling modifier by putting "Jenna" — the person who is exhausted — immediately after the introductory phrase.
- **C9 — A) how to give effective presentations.** Matches the parallel "how to ___" structure of the first two list items.
- **C10 — A) who.** The clause is essential (it identifies *which* employees get the certificate — not all of them), so no commas and "who" (not "whom," which would need to be an object, not the subject of "complete").
- **C11 — A) :.** "The chef's philosophy was simple" is a complete independent clause, so a colon can introduce the explanation that follows.
